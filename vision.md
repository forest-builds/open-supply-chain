Yes. This direction is structurally strong.

Government compliance services have the kind of demand profile that makes for durable businesses:

- The customer does not want the product.
- The customer must buy the outcome.
- The rules change often enough to create recurring work.
- Mistakes are expensive.
- The buyer is usually relieved to outsource it.

That is a good wedge for open-supply-chain, especially if the platform becomes infrastructure for answering: “What obligations apply to this organization, facility, shipment, material, supplier, or route?”

The sharper version might be:

**Compliance intelligence and filing infrastructure for regulated supply chains.**

Not just “we help you stay compliant,” but:

> “We know which rules apply to your supply chain, what evidence you need, what filings are due, and what risk changes when your suppliers, facilities, routes, or products change.”

Potential service lines:

1. **Environmental compliance**
   - Emissions reporting
   - Waste handling
   - Water discharge permits
   - Facility-level environmental obligations
   - Supplier environmental documentation

2. **Trade and customs compliance**
   - Import/export restrictions
   - Sanctions screening
   - Forced labor compliance
   - Country-of-origin documentation
   - Tariff classification support

3. **Safety and incident reporting**
   - OSHA-style reporting
   - Hazardous materials handling
   - Facility safety audits
   - Recall and incident workflows

4. **Permits and renewals**
   - Facility permits
   - Operating licenses
   - Transportation permits
   - Local/state/federal renewal calendars

5. **Regulatory change monitoring**
   - “This new rule affects these 12 facilities and 43 suppliers.”
   - “These filings are now required.”
   - “This product/material is newly exposed.”

The really interesting product thesis is that compliance is not document-first. It is graph-first.

A regulation applies because of relationships:

- company operates facility
- facility handles material
- material crosses border
- supplier is in jurisdiction
- product contains component
- route touches port
- port is subject to inspection regime
- organization has reporting threshold

That maps beautifully onto supply-chain intelligence.

A possible wedge customer:

**Mid-market manufacturers/importers who lack large compliance teams but face complex trade, environmental, and supplier-risk obligations.**

They are big enough to feel pain, small enough to buy help, and often stuck between spreadsheets, consultants, and government portals.

The risk: “government compliance” is huge and mushy. You would need to pick one painful lane first.

Best initial wedges, in my view:

1. **Forced labor / supplier compliance for importers**
   Very supply-chain native, high board/legal concern, evidence-heavy.

2. **Environmental facility compliance for manufacturers**
   Recurring, document-heavy, local/state/federal complexity.

3. **Customs/trade compliance monitoring**
   Directly tied to shipments, suppliers, ports, routes, and products.

The tagline could be something like:

> **Compliance operations for complex supply chains.**

Or more concrete:

> **Know what rules apply, what evidence is missing, and what filings are due.**

This feels much closer to a buyer with budget than a broad “supply-chain map” alone. The map becomes the substrate; compliance becomes the paid workflow.
